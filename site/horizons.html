Open questions / 5 September 2026

Leave room for
different lives.

The fly connectome is a beginning. We are interested in the ecologies that could grow around it: changing bodies, inherited environments and relationships with consequences. These are directions for experiments, open to revision.

01 / An inherited world

Can a habitat become an ancestor’s work?

A branch can cast shade, a wall can redirect transport, and a hollow can shelter a later inhabitant. Construction lets one life change the conditions of another. The inherited environment could matter alongside inherited genes and personal experience.

Resource-funded growth already produces physical geometry. The next challenge is joining its material to mobile organisms’ acquisition, maintenance and reproduction. We want to discover when investment in a durable structure pays for its costs, and when that structure creates opportunities for other ways of living.

Compare descendants raised among ancestral structures, cleared terrain and unfamiliar structures, while matching their starting resources. Which differences survive a move to a common environment?

02 / Social ecologies

What could make care worth learning?

A shared nursery could contain young that need material, protection or transport before becoming independent. A resident spending time there gives up other opportunities. If care changes actual development and later outcomes, we can investigate the conditions under which it persists.

We would supply the mechanisms of reproduction and dependency, then investigate behavior: who approaches, transfers resources, repairs a shelter or leaves? Signals should travel through senses. Care should consume real resources. Task preferences should be able to change with experience and local demand.

Eusociality is a more specific target than cooperation. Conventional criteria include cooperative brood care, overlapping adult generations and reproductive division of labor, although definitions differ. The current runtime has not established these criteria; embodied reproduction and dependent young remain missing mechanisms.

Definition and its limits: Crespi & Yanega, 1995. Flexible task allocation provides a useful experimental precedent: Robinson, Feinerman & Franks, 2009.

Does care persist when cues and locations change? Does group performance depend on communication, material exchange or simply having more individuals and resources?

03 / Coordination through surroundings

Could building become a conversation?

An organism can respond to a structure another left behind without identifying its builder. Geometry, chemical traces and the presence of other builders could each influence what happens next. Their effects are separable.

Ant construction research motivates experiments with body dimensions and chemical templates. Termite experiments also point to aggregation at active excavation sites as an alternative to a presumed cement-pheromone rule. We should preserve these competing explanations in our own world.

Khuong et al., 2016; Green et al., 2017.

Keep a built shape but remove its chemical trace; preserve the trace but replace the shape; change who is present. What actually guides the next construction?

04 / A changing nervous system

What should descendants be able to change?

Related organisms could allocate different amounts to sensing, mobility, tissue, digestion and neural processing. The environment determines what those investments permit. A long reach may help access a surface and make another passage difficult; a large photosynthetic surface also needs material and maintenance.

We have begun with constrained heritable chemistry, developmental geometry and mobile body parameters. The measured fly graph remains a reference substrate. Derived circuit architectures need explicit ancestry, sensory and motor interfaces, and their own tests of useful behavior.

More ambitious descendants might express, duplicate or specialize neural modules together with the organs they serve. A synthetic transferable developmental module could also provide a shared connection between lineages. Such a carrier would have declared costs and effects in the world; shared model weights alone do not create an ecological relationship.

Do altered bodies and circuits acquire complementary capabilities under different conditions? Can those differences persist without a supplied species label directing behavior?

05 / People at the boundary

Can we acquire habits together?

A person can offer movement, sound, objects and changes to a habitat. Residents encounter these through limited senses. The interesting possibility is mutual adjustment: a person learns an individual’s timing while that individual learns something about the encounters.

The archive can record an observation such as “she waited for the second note,” alongside alternatives such as timing, location or resource history. A controlled comparison can then change one of those conditions. The story remains connected to evidence without becoming a hidden instruction to the creature.

Do learned responses transfer to new examples and places? Can observers distinguish individuals when names are hidden and immediate physiological states are balanced?

A notebook can change its mind.

These questions guide implementation without fixing the answers. We will keep source revisions, unsuccessful trials and simpler competing models visible. Interesting coordination, useful cognition and subjective experience are different claims; a compelling visualization settles none of them.
